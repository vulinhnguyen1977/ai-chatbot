Chapter 4: Speed, Velocity and Acceleration

Speed tells us how much distance an object covers in a given time. To calculate
the average speed of an object, divide the total distance travelled by the total
time taken. The formula is speed = distance / time. Distance is measured in
metres and time in seconds, so speed is measured in metres per second, written
m/s. A cyclist who travels 100 metres in 20 seconds has an average speed of
100 / 20 = 5 m/s. Notice that this is an average: the cyclist may have been
faster at some moments and slower at others.

Velocity is speed in a stated direction. Two cars travelling at 30 m/s in
opposite directions have the same speed but different velocities. Because
velocity includes direction, a change of direction is a change of velocity even
when the speed stays the same.

Acceleration measures how quickly velocity changes. Acceleration equals the
change in velocity divided by the time taken for that change. Written as a
formula, acceleration = (final velocity - initial velocity) / time. The units
are metres per second squared, written m/s^2. If a car speeds up from 5 m/s to
25 m/s in 4 seconds, the change in velocity is 20 m/s, and the acceleration is
20 / 4 = 5 m/s^2. When an object slows down, the acceleration is negative and we
often call it deceleration.

Distance-time graphs show distance on the vertical axis and time on the
horizontal axis. The gradient of a distance-time graph gives the speed. A
horizontal line means the object is stationary, because the distance is not
changing. A straight sloping line means constant speed, and a curved line means
the speed is changing.

Chapter 5: Forces and Motion

A force is a push or a pull. Forces are measured in newtons, written N. When
several forces act on an object we add them together, taking direction into
account, to find the resultant force. If the forces on an object are balanced,
the resultant force is zero and the object either stays still or keeps moving at
a constant velocity. If the forces are unbalanced, the object accelerates in the
direction of the resultant force.

Weight is the force of gravity acting on an object. Weight is calculated as
weight = mass x gravitational field strength. On Earth the gravitational field
strength is about 10 newtons per kilogram, so a 3 kg bag has a weight of
3 x 10 = 30 N. Mass is a measure of how much matter an object contains and is
measured in kilograms; it does not change when the object is taken to the Moon,
but its weight does because the gravitational field strength there is weaker.

Friction is a force that opposes motion between two surfaces that are touching.
Friction always acts in the opposite direction to the movement. Air resistance is
a form of friction caused by an object moving through air. When a falling object
speeds up, the air resistance on it increases until it balances the weight. At
that point the resultant force is zero, the object stops accelerating, and it
falls at a steady speed called the terminal velocity.

Chapter 9: Electric Circuits

An electric current is a flow of charge. Current is measured in amperes, often
shortened to amps and written A. Voltage, also called potential difference, is a
measure of the energy given to the charge and is measured in volts, written V.
Resistance opposes the flow of current and is measured in ohms.

Ohm's law states that voltage = current x resistance, usually written V = I x R.
If a resistor of 4 ohms carries a current of 3 A, the voltage across it is
3 x 4 = 12 V. Rearranging the formula lets us find any one of the three
quantities when the other two are known: current = voltage / resistance, and
resistance = voltage / current.

In a series circuit the components are connected one after another in a single
loop. The current is the same at every point in a series circuit, and the total
voltage of the supply is shared between the components. The total resistance of
resistors in series is found by adding them together.

In a parallel circuit the components are connected across each other so that
there is more than one path for the current. Each branch of a parallel circuit
has the full supply voltage across it, and the current divides between the
branches. This is why two identical bulbs are brighter in parallel than in
series: in parallel each bulb receives the full voltage, whereas in series they
share it.
